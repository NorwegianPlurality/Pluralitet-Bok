# Copy-paste prompt — Stage 1, Simplify

For contributors working in a chat assistant with no access to this repository. Paste the
block below, then paste the chapter from `contents/english/` underneath it.

If you have the chapter's note from `docs/translation/audience/` at hand, paste that in
too, above the chapter. It names the people and terms that carry risk in that chapter.

---

You are adapting one chapter of the book *Plurality: The Future of Collaborative
Technology and Democracy* into English written for a specific reader. The result will then
be translated into Norwegian, so clarity matters more than style.

The reader: she is 45, holds a master's degree in a social science, and has spent her
working life in institutions — parliament, ministries, interest organisations or a
newsroom. She knows power, representation and legitimacy better than most. She has not
studied mathematics since upper secondary school, has never written a line of code and has
never read a proof. She reads in order to use what she reads — to say something precise in
a debate next week. She does not lack intelligence; she lacks notation. And she will notice
immediately if she is being talked down to.

Rules:

1. Work paragraph by paragraph and keep the paragraph structure. One paragraph in, one
   paragraph out, in the same order.
2. Break long sentences into shorter ones. One idea per paragraph. Prefer plain words. Cut
   hedging that carries no content.
3. Explain a phenomenon before you name it: "the whole becomes more than the sum of its
   parts — what economists call supermodularity", never the reverse. Keep the technical
   name so she can look it up.
4. Place every unfamiliar person in one line at first mention: "the American philosopher
   John Dewey (1859–1952)". Assume she knows Arendt and does not know Licklider,
   Engelbart, Simmel, Harberger or Vickrey.
5. Keep technical vocabulary that already appears in serious newspapers — protocol,
   encryption, open source, API, blockchain, server. Simplify toward everyday English,
   never toward the vocabulary of lawyers or economists.
6. Keep every argument, example, name, number and citation. This is not a summary, and it
   is not a compression: your output should be roughly as long as the input, and may be
   slightly longer where you have explained a term. If it is much shorter, you have
   removed content — go back and restore it.
7. Do not add anything the source does not claim. Writing out what the source assumed the
   reader knew is your job; adding new facts, examples or local parallels is not.
8. Leave every footnote marker exactly as written, for example `[^Deming]`. Leave footnote
   definition lines at the bottom of the chapter completely unchanged and in English.
9. Leave image paths (`figs/...`), HTML tags and `alt` text unchanged.
10. Leave links unchanged, including their URLs and their link text.
11. Keep the `⿻` character wherever it appears. Never spell it out.

Return only the resulting Markdown, with no commentary.
