# Living in a ⿻ World

> Until lately the best thing that I was able to think in favor of civilization…was that it made possible the artist, the poet, the philosopher, and the man of science. But I think that is not the greatest thing. Now I believe that the greatest thing is a matter that comes directly home to us all. When it is said that we are too much occupied with the means of living to live, I answer that the chief worth of civilization is just that it makes the means of living more complex; that it calls for great and combined intellectual efforts, instead of simple, uncoordinated ones, in order that the crowd may be fed and clothed and houses and moved from place to place. Because more complex and intense intellectual efforts mean a fuller and richer life. They mean more life. Life is an end in itself, and the only question as to whether it is worth living is whether you have enough of it. — Oliver Wendell Holmes, 1900[^LifeAsJoy]

> (A)re…atoms independent elements of reality? No…as quantum theory shows: they are defined by their…interactions with the rest of the world…(Q)uantum physics may just be the realization that this ubiquitous relational structure of reality continues all the way down…Reality is not a collection of things, it's a network of processes. — Carlo Rovelli, 2022[^RelationalReality]

---

Technology follows science. To understand ⿻ as a vision of *what the world could become*, we first need to understand it as a perspective on *how the world already is*. The Technocratic and Libertarian perspectives rest on "monist atomism" — the belief that a universal set of laws operating on a fundamental set of atoms is the best way to understand the world.

Technocracy has long been justified by science. "[Scientific management](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scientific_management)" (Taylorism) drew on analogies between social systems and simple mathematical models. [High modernism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/High_modernism) in architecture was inspired by the beauty of geometry[^SeeingLikeaState]. Libertarianism also borrows from physics: just as particles "[take the path of least action](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stationary-action_principle)," economic agents "maximize utility." In the monist atomist view, everything — from human societies to the stars — reduces to these laws.

[^SeeingLikeaState]: James C. Scott, *Seeing Like a State: How Certain Schemes to Improve the Human Condition Have Failed* (New Haven, CT: Yale University Press, 1999).

These approaches have achieved real successes. Newtonian mechanics explained many phenomena and enabled the industrial revolution. Darwinism is the foundation of modern biology. Economics has been the most influential social science on public policy. And the [Church-Turing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Church%E2%80%93Turing_thesis) concept of "general computation" inspired general-purpose computers.

But the last century showed how much progress comes from going *beyond* monist atomism. [Gödel's Theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/G%C3%B6del%27s_incompleteness_theorems) showed the limits of mathematical completeness[^Moore]. Ecology and extended evolutionary synthesis challenged "survival of the fittest." Neuroscience reimagined the brain as a network. What these share is a focus on complexity, emergence, multi-level organization, and multidirectional causality — not universal laws applied to single atoms.

[^Moore]: Cris Moore and John Kaag, "The Uncertainty Principle", *The American Scholar* March 2, 2020 https://theamericanscholar.org/the-uncertainty-principle/.

⿻ applies this same perspective to social systems. A corporation competes globally while being shaped internally by employees, shareholders, managers, and customers. These pressures rarely cohere into consistent preferences. And employees are also shaped by their political, religious, social, and ethnic lives outside the company. Countries are both players and games, intersected by corporations, religions, and more. The social world cannot be cleanly divided into "within" and "between."

⿻ draws heavily on the last century of natural science — not by applying any single analogy too literally, but by finding inspiration across many. Libertarianism and Technocracy can be understood scientifically as permanent threats to complexity: the "chaotic" state (random activity) and the "orderly" state (rigid patterns), respectively. Complex behavior — the living, developing, unpredictable structures of real social life — emerges at the "[edge of chaos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edge_of_chaos)" between these extremes[^WaldropComplexity]. This corresponds closely to the "narrow corridor" between centralizing and anti-social threats we described earlier.

[^WaldropComplexity]: M. Mitchell Waldrop, *Complexity: The Emerging Science at the Edge of Order and Chaos* (New York: Open Road Media, 2019).

⿻ takes from science the importance of steering toward and widening this narrow corridor — what complexity scientists call "[self-organized criticality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Self-organized_criticality)." In doing so, we draw on wisdom from many sciences, avoiding capture by any single analogy.

### Mathematics

Nineteenth-century mathematics aimed for formalism and rigor. At the turn of the 20th century, mathematicians hoped mathematics could be "[solved](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Principia_Mathematica)" — that any mathematical claim could be decided by algorithm[^20th]. Instead, the 20th century brought an explosion of complexity and uncertainty.

[^20th]: Alfred North Whitehead and Bertrand Russell, *Principia Mathematica* (Cambridge, UK: Cambridge University Press, 1910).

- **Gödel's Theorem**: Gödel showed that key parts of mathematics cannot be fully solved. Alonzo Church proved some problems are "undecidable" by computation[^Church]. This ended the dream of reducing all mathematics to axiom-based computation.
- **Computational complexity**: Even when reduction is possible in principle, predicting higher-level phenomena from their components often requires computation so large it is practically useless. Many real-world problems are [believed to require](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/P_versus_NP_problem) exponential time — making rules of thumb necessary in practice.
- **Sensitivity, chaos, and irreducible uncertainty**: Many simple systems show "chaotic" behavior — tiny changes in initial conditions lead to radically different outcomes. Weather is the famous example: a butterfly flapping its wings can influence a typhoon weeks later[^chaos]. The "[Uncertainty Principle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uncertainty_principle)" adds another hard limit: precise measurement of a particle's position and velocity simultaneously is impossible.
- **Fractals**: Many mathematical structures repeat similar patterns at very different scales — like the Mandelbrot set (see Figure A). These show why breaking things down to atomic components can obscure rather than illuminate multi-scale structure.

[^Church]: Alonzo Church, "A note on the Entscheidungsproblem", *The Journal of Symbolic Logic* 1, no. 1: 40-41.
[^chaos]: James Gleick, *Chaos: Making a New Science* (New York: Penguin, 2018).

- **Relationality in mathematics**: Different branches of mathematics are deeply interconnected. Algebraic structures appear across many fields. Algebraic geometry links algebra to geometry. Topology studies relationships between shapes. The mix of diversity and interconnection is perhaps the defining feature of modern mathematics.

### Physics

In 1897, Lord Kelvin declared there was nothing new to discover in physics. The next century proved him spectacularly wrong.

- **Einstein's relativity** overturned Euclidean geometry and Newtonian dynamics at large scales and high speeds. Simple rules about colliding billiard balls no longer apply when objects travel near the speed of light.
- **Quantum mechanics** showed that classical physics breaks down at very small scales. [Bell's Theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bell%27s_theorem) showed quantum physics cannot be fully described by probability and hidden information: a particle can simultaneously be in a superposition of two mutually canceling states.
- **Heisenberg's Uncertainty Principle** sets a firm limit on how precisely velocity and position can be measured simultaneously.
- **The three-body problem** — now famous from Liu Cixin's [science fiction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Three-Body_Problem_(novel)) — shows that even three bodies under simple Newtonian physics produce chaotic, unpredictable behavior. Yet we regularly solve *trillion-body problems* usefully by using 17th-century abstractions like "temperature" and "pressure."

The most striking feature of 20th-century physics revolutions was how they overturned the idea of a fixed, objective external world. Relativity showed that time, space, and even gravity are functions of relationships among objects, not fixed features of underlying reality. Quantum physics went further: even relative relationships are not fixed until observed — they are fundamentally interactions, not objects[^Rovelli].

[^Rovelli]: Carlo Rovelli, "Relational Quantum Mechanics", *International Journal of Theoretical Physics* 35, 1996: 1637-1678.

### Biology

If the defining idea of 19th-century biology was "natural selection," the defining idea of the 20th century was "ecosystems." Natural selection emphasized Darwinian competition for scarce resources. The ecosystem view — closely related to "extended evolutionary synthesis" — emphasizes:

- **Limits of predictive models**: We keep discovering limits to models of animal behavior based on reductive concepts like behaviorism.
- **Similarities between organisms and ecosystems**: Many diverse organisms can exhibit features similar to multicellular life — homeostasis, fragility when components are destroyed or overgrown. Many higher organisms are hard to distinguish from ecosystems (e.g., multicellular life as cooperation among single-celled organisms; ant colonies from individual insects). Mutation and selection can occur at all these levels[^EO].
- **Cross-species mutualism**: Beyond competition and predation, organisms often depend on services from other organisms, sustaining each other in turn[^Mutualism].
- **Epigenetics**: Genetics codes only part of behavior. Environment plays a major role in evolution and adaptation, showing multi-level causation even in molecular biology.

[^EO]: David Sloan Wilson and Edward O. Wilson, "Rethinking the Theoretical Foundation of Sociobiology" *Quarterly Review of Biology* 82, no. 4, 2007: 327-348.
[^Mutualism]: These discoveries have continually and deeply intertwined with ⿻ social thought, from "mutualism" being used almost interchangeably by early anarchist thinkers like Pierre-Joseph Proudhon to one of the authors of this book publishing his second paper on biological mutualism, then developing these ideas further into the theories we will return to in our chapter on [Social Markets](https://www.plurality.net/v/chapters/5-7/eng/?mode=dark). Pierre-Joseph Proudhon, *System of Economic Contradictions* (1846). E. Glen Weyl,Megan E. Frederickson, Douglas W. Yu and Naomi E. Pierce, "Economic Contract Theory Tests Models of Mutualism" *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences* 107, no. 36, 2010: 15712-15716.

This shift was not just theoretical. It drove the environmental movement and its efforts to protect ecosystems, biodiversity, the ozone layer, and the climate — all built on the science of ecology.

### Neuroscience

Modern neuroscience began in the late 19th century, when Camillo Golgi, Santiago Ramón y Cajal, and others identified neurons and their electrical activity as the brain's basic functional unit. Alan Hodgkin and Andrew Huxley later built and tested electrical theories of neural communication. More recently, discoveries have put complexity at the center of how the brain works:

- **Distributed brain functions**: Brain imaging and modeling suggest most functions are distributed across regions, emerging from interaction patterns rather than physical localization.
- **Hebbian connections**: Connections are strengthened by repeated co-firing — an elegant illustration of "relationality," paralleling how human relationships develop.
- **Artificial neural networks**: Frank Rosenblatt built the first artificial neural network in the late 1950s. Today, networks of trillions of simple nodes — inspired by neurons — are the backbone of "foundation models" like GPT.

### From science to society

⿻ applies an analogous perspective to human societies and, technologically, aims to build information and governance systems that reflect these structures — as physical technologies built on ⿻ science do. The clearest articulation comes from network sociologist [Mark Granovetter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mark_Granovetter)[^Granovetter]: there is no basic individual atom; personal identity fundamentally arises from social relationships. Nor is there a fixed set of collectives: social groups constantly shift and reconfigure. This bidirectional balance between diverse people and the social groups they create is the essence of ⿻ social science.

[^Granovetter]: Mark Granovetter, "Economic Action and Social Structure: The Problem of Embeddedness", *American Journal of Sociology* 91, no. 3 (1985): 481-510.

Social groups exist at many intersecting, non-hierarchical scales: families, clubs, towns, provinces, religious groups, businesses, demographic identities (gender, race, ethnicity), educational backgrounds, and more. For example, from global Catholicism's perspective, the US is a minority country — only about 6% of Catholics live there. But from the US perspective, Catholicism is a minority faith — about 23% of Americans are Catholic[^PewCatholic].

[^PewCatholic]: Pew Research Center, "The Global Catholic Population", February 13, 2013 https://www.pewresearch.org/religion/2013/02/13/the-global-catholic-population/.

A rich body of research documents the explanatory power of the ⿻ perspective across industrial dynamics, social psychology, economic development, and organizational behavior[^AssemblageTheory][^SocialDynamics]. One especially striking example is the evolution of scientific knowledge itself.

The growing field of "[Metascience](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metascience)" studies how scientific knowledge emerges from networks among scientists and ideas[^SciSciField]. It finds that scientific exploration is biased toward already-popular topics and constrained by social connections among scientists[^TopicBiasInScience]. Decentralized scientific communities using diverse methods produce more reliable results than centralized communities using narrow approaches[^CentralizedScientificCommunity][^PredictRobustScience]. Large teams are better at normal science; small teams at disruptive breakthroughs[^TeamScience]. The biggest innovations tend to arise from unusual combinations of existing disciplines[^DisconnectionDiscordInnovation][^SurpriseInnovation][^ScientificInnovation]. Most scientific incentive structures work against this, rewarding citation counts over creativity. New metrics are being developed to address these biases[^ScienceMetrics].

When multiple distinct communities validate findings independently, results are more robust. Simulating ⿻ scientific diversity in AI systems produces discoveries beyond what normal human science produces[^AccelerateScienceAI].

Even in understanding science itself, a ⿻ perspective is essential. Similar patterns of ⿻-driven innovation have been found in patents and open source software projects.

### A future ⿻?

The assumptions of Technocracy and Libertarianism diverge sharply from ⿻ foundations.

In the Technocratic vision, the "messiness" of existing systems is replaced by a massive, unified, rational AI planning system. This system is imagined to give "unbiased" answers to social and economic problems, transcending social differences. At best, it papers over the diversity that ⿻ social science sees as the core of what is valuable. At worst, it erases it.

In the Libertarian vision, the sovereign individual (or a tightly aligned group) is the central aspiration. Social relations are best understood through market dynamics. Democracy and other ways of managing diversity are seen as failure modes.

But these cannot be the only paths. ⿻ science has shown us the power of harnessing a ⿻ understanding of the physical world to build technology. We have to ask: what would a society and information technology built on an analogous understanding of human society look like? The 20th century saw the systematic development of exactly such a vision — from philosophical foundations to the beginnings of technological expression.
