Living in Taiwan makes it easy to take its civic digital achievements for granted. For outsiders, it is hard to grasp what they mean. Here we offer concrete examples and data that show what distinguishes Taiwan's digital civic infrastructure from the rest of the world. There are far too many examples to cover in detail, so we have selected six — roughly one per two-year period since 2012 — followed by a brief list of other programs.

### Illustrations

#### g0v

More than any other institution, [g0v](https://g0v.tw/) (pronounced "gov-zero") represents the civil-society foundation of digital democracy in Taiwan. Founded in 2012 by civic hackers including Kao Chia-liang, g0v emerged from frustration with poor government digital services and limited data transparency[^g0vManifesto]. Hackers began scraping government websites (at gov.tw) and building better versions, hosted at g0v.tw. These "forked" government websites often became more popular than the originals, leading ministers like Simon Chang to "merge" these designs back into official services.

[^g0vManifesto]: [g0v Manifesto](https://g0v.tw/intl/en/manifesto/en/) defines it as "a non-partisan, not-for-profit, grassroots movement". [MoeDict](https://moedict.tw/), an early g0v project, was led by one of the authors of this book.

g0v built on this success to grow a vibrant community of civic hackers working with non-technical civil society groups at regular events called "[jothons](https://jothon.g0v.tw/about/en/)" (a Mandarin play on words meaning roughly "join-a-thon"). Unlike typical hackathons, g0v's events feature a mostly non-technical majority with nearly full gender parity, a focus on civic problems rather than commercial outcomes, and close collaboration with civil society. The community's motto — "Ask not why nobody is doing this. You are the 'nobody'!" — earned it the name "nobody movement," illustrated by the Venn diagram in Figure A. Most initiatives we highlight below grew from g0v and closely aligned projects.

#### Sunflower

g0v gained public attention early, but burst into the spotlight during the Sunflower Movement. Hundreds of g0v contributors supported the occupation of the Legislative Yuan — broadcasting, documenting, and communicating civic actions. Livestreams sparked public debate. Street vendors, lawyers, teachers, and designers joined online and offline. Digital tools helped crowdfunding, rallies, and international support.

On March 30, 2014, half a million people marched in the largest demonstration in Taiwan since the 1980s. Their demand — a review process before passage of the Cross-Straits Services Trade Agreement — was accepted by Legislative Yuan Speaker Wang Jin-pyng on April 6, about three weeks after the occupation began. The government came to respect g0v's methods. Cabinet member Jaclyn Tsai recruited one of us as a youth "reverse mentor" and began attending and supporting g0v meetings, putting more government materials into the public domain through g0v platforms.

Many Sunflower participants devoted themselves to the open government movement. The 2014 local and 2016 general elections saw roughly a 10-percentage-point swing toward the Green camp, and a new political party — the New Power Party — was founded by Sunflower leaders, including rock star Freddy Lim. These events added momentum behind g0v and led to one of us being appointed Minister without Portfolio for open government, social innovation, and youth participation.

#### vTaiwan and Join

As g0v's methods became institutionalized, there was growing demand to apply them to a wider range of policy issues. This led to [vTaiwan](https://vtaiwan.tw/intro/), a platform and project developed by g0v for deliberating on public policy controversies. The process involves multiple steps — proposal, opinion expression, reflection, and legislation — each using open source tools. It became best known for its use of Polis, a machine-learning-based open-source "wikisurvey" tool (then novel in 2015) that we discuss in our chapter on [Augmented Deliberation](https://www.plurality.net/v/chapters/5-4/eng/?mode=dark). Polis works like a microblogging service, but uses dimension reduction to cluster opinions as shown in Figure B. Instead of maximizing engagement, it shows opinion clusters and highlights statements that bridge them — supporting both consensus and clearer understanding of divisions.

vTaiwan was designed as an intensive, high-touch platform for committed participants. It reached about 200,000 users — around 1% of Taiwan's population — and held detailed deliberations on 28 issues, 80% of which led to legislative action. Topics included ride-sharing regulation, non-consensual intimate images, financial technology experimentation, and AI regulation.

As a decentralized, citizen-led community, vTaiwan has evolved over time. Participation declined during COVID-19, which disrupted in-person meetings. Challenges included the heavy volunteer burden and lack of mandatory government responses. In response, the community sought a new role between public and government. A key revival effort was its collaboration with OpenAI's Democratic Input to AI project in 2023 — partnering with Chatham House on deliberative events about AI ethics, bringing local perspectives into global AI governance discussions. Looking ahead, vTaiwan plans to work on AI-related regulation in Taiwan and beyond, experimenting with new deliberation tools and integrating LLMs for summarization.

The Public Digital Innovation Space (PDIS), established in 2016, supported both vTaiwan and a second platform, [Join](https://join.gov.tw/). Join has a lighter interface, focuses on gathering input and initiatives from a broader public, and requires officials to respond to proposals that receive sufficient support. Unlike vTaiwan, Join covers all policy areas — including non-technology issues like high school start times — and has strong ongoing usage: roughly half of Taiwan's population over its lifetime and an average of 11,000 unique daily visitors.

#### Hackathons, coalitions and quadratic signals

Such levels of digital civic engagement can be seen as redirecting the energy wasted on social media conflict toward solving public problems. The Presidential Hackathon (PH) took this further by placing government support behind g0v's hackathon model.

The PH brought together mixed teams of civil servants, academics, activists, and technologists to propose tools, social practices, and collective data arrangements. Teams could "collectively bargain" with their data for cooperation with government and private actors through the government's "data coalitions" program. Examples included air quality monitoring and wildfire early warning systems.

Participants and citizens voted on winners using Quadratic Voting — a system that allows people to express the strength of their preferences across multiple projects, as we discuss in our [⿻ Voting](https://www.plurality.net/v/chapters/5-6/eng/?mode=dark) chapter. This made it likely that everyone would support at least some winner, and allowed voters who felt strongly about a project to give it extra weight. Winners received a trophy — a microprojector showing the President of Taiwan presenting the award — which gave them leverage to get relevant government agencies or localities to cooperate, given the legitimacy g0v had built.

More recently, this model expanded to "ideathons" that envision alternative futures and produce media content. It also moved from symbolic support to real funding for valued projects — such as agricultural and food safety inspection tools — using an extension of Quadratic Voting to Funding, as we discuss in our [Social Markets](https://www.plurality.net/v/chapters/5-7/eng/?mode=dark) chapter.

#### Pandemic

These approaches to agile, civil-society-driven governance were tested most dramatically during COVID-19. Taiwan is widely considered to have had one of the world's most effective early pandemic responses. It achieved among the lowest global death rates without lockdowns, while maintaining among the fastest economic growth rates in the world. Being an island helped, as did having an epidemiologist as Vice-President and restricting travel early. But digital tools also played an important role.

The best-documented example was the "Mask App." Building on experience with SARS, civic hackers led by Howard Wu developed an app that drew on government open data — shaped by g0v's transparency practices — to map mask availability in real time. Taiwan achieved widespread mask adoption by mid-February, even as global supply remained tight.

Another key element was rigorous testing, tracing, and supported isolation. Taiwan was among the few places able to achieve the adoption rates needed to make phone-based contact tracing effective. This worked largely because of close cooperation between government health officials and g0v community members, who were deeply concerned about privacy — especially given Taiwan's lack of an independent privacy protection regime. The result was a system with strong anonymization and decentralization features that won broad public acceptance.

#### Information integrity

Taiwan's single most important digital contribution to pandemic response may have been its ability to rapidly counter misinformation and deliberate disinformation. This capability has extended well beyond the pandemic and proved critical to successful elections at a time when information integrity challenges have troubled many other countries.

Central to this is the g0v spin-off project "Cofacts," where citizens rapidly respond to trending social media content and to private messages forwarded to a public comment box. [Recent research](https://tech.cornell.edu/news/crowdsourced-fact-checking-fights-misinformation-in-taiwan/) shows these systems typically respond faster, with equal accuracy and more engagement, than professional fact-checkers — who are far more bandwidth-constrained[^CornellTech].

[^CornellTech]: Andy Zhao and Mor Naaman, "Insights from a Comparative Study on the Variety, Velocity, Veracity, and Viability of Crowdsourced and Professional Fact-Checking Services", *Journal of Online Trust and Safety* 2, no. 1. https://doi.org/10.54501/jots.v2i1.118.

Taiwan's technically sophisticated civil sector and public support have enabled other tools as well: chatbots from MyGoPen and Gogolook for private messaging apps like Line that give citizens fast, anonymous responses to misleading claims. Government leaders' close cooperation with civil groups has allowed them to model "humor over rumor" responses — "fast, fun and fair." When a pandemic rumor claimed masks were causing a toilet paper shortage, Premier Su Tseng-chang famously circulated a picture of himself wagging his rear to signal there was nothing to fear.

Together these policies have helped Taiwan fight the "infodemic" without takedowns — just as it fought the pandemic without lockdowns. The January 13, 2024 election was the culmination: an unprecedented, AI-enhanced PRC disinformation campaign failed to polarize or noticeably shift the vote.

#### Other programs

Many other Taiwanese digital democracy initiatives deserve mention:

1. [Alignment assemblies](https://moda.gov.tw/en/major-policies/alignment-assemblies/1453): Taiwan has pioneered citizen participation in the regulation and steering of AI foundation models.
2. **Information security**: Taiwan is a world leader in using distributed storage against malicious takedowns and "[zero trust](https://www.trade.gov/country-commercial-guides/taiwan-cybersecurity)" principles for citizen account security.
3. [Gold cards](https://goldcard.nat.gov.tw/en/): Taiwan's "gold card" program offers diverse paths to permanent residence, including a "digital field" for those who have contributed to open source and public interest software.
4. **Transparency**: One of us has made recordings and transcripts of all her official meetings publicly available without copyright.
5. **Digital competence education**: Since 2019, Taiwan's 12-Year Basic Education Curriculum enshrines "tech, info & media literacy" as a core competency, empowering students as active co-creators and critical consumers of media.
6. **Land and spectrum**: Building on Henry George's ideas, Taiwan has innovative policies to ensure full utilization of land and electromagnetic spectrum through taxes including compulsory-sale rights, as we discuss in our [Property and Contract](https://www.plurality.net/v/chapters/4-4/eng/?mode=dark) and [Social Markets](https://www.plurality.net/v/chapters/5-7/eng/?mode=dark) chapters.
7. [Participation Officer Network](https://po.pdis.nat.gov.tw/en/po/): PDIS created a network of civil servants across departments committed to citizen participation, cross-department collaboration, and digital feedback.
8. **Broadband access**: Taiwan has one of the most universal internet access rates and has been ranked the world's fastest average internet two years running.
9. **Open parliament**: Taiwan is a leader in the global "open parliament" movement, making parliamentary procedures transparent and experimenting with innovative voting methods.
10. **Digital diplomacy**: Taiwan has become a leading advisor to democracies worldwide facing similar challenges and seeking to use digital tools to improve participation and resilience.

This work earned sufficient public and government confidence that in August 2022, Taiwan created a Ministry of Digital Affairs, elevating one of us from Minister without Portfolio to lead the new ministry.

### Decade of accomplishment

These programs are interesting, but what matters is whether they worked. Tracing precise causal impacts is beyond our scope here. At minimum, though, we can ask how Taiwan has performed overall on the challenges that have troubled most liberal democracies. (Note: Taiwan's complex geopolitical status means many standard international datasets exclude it, limiting the comparisons possible.)

#### Economic

Taiwan's GDP per capita is about $34,000 (2024, IMF)[^IMFgdp]. Adjusted for purchasing power, it is the second richest country with more than 10 million people, after the US. Combined with relatively low inequality, Taiwan likely has the world's highest typical living standards for a country of its size.

Taiwan's economy is unusually tech-intensive. Electronics and ICT exports account for roughly 31% of GDP — more than twice the share in other leading tech exporters like Israel and South Korea[^TradingEcon]. Taiwan is best known as the world's primary manufacturer of advanced semiconductors, and as the home of smartphone manufacturers like Foxconn.

Taiwan also has a low tax burden — 11% of GDP versus an OECD average of 34%[^taxtake] — and ranks 4th in the Heritage Foundation's Economic Freedom Index[^EconFreedom].

Against this background, Taiwan's recent economic performance is notable:

1. **Growth**: Taiwan averaged 3% real GDP growth over the last decade, versus under 2% for the OECD and about 2% for the US[^WB].
2. **Unemployment**: Taiwan averaged just under 4% unemployment, versus 6% for the OECD and 5% for the US.
3. **Inflation**: Taiwan's inflation stayed in the 0–2% range over the last decade, averaging 1.3%, while most rich countries saw sharp spikes and volatility.
4. **Inequality**: Traditional surveys show Taiwan's Gini coefficient steady at about .28 — around Austria's level, and far below the US at roughly .4. More recent administrative methods by economists like Piketty and Saez show Taiwan's top 1% income share at 19%, not far behind the US (21%). However, these methods may overstate inequality in low-tax countries like Taiwan[^Inequalitycritique]. In any case, Taiwan's top 1% share fell by about a tenth in the last decade, while it rose in both France and the US.

The key point: Taiwan is the world's most tech-intensive economy, and yet lacks the slow growth, high unemployment, and rising inequality that economists have blamed on technology elsewhere[^CapitalShare].

#### Social

International social data for Taiwan are limited due to its exclusion from the WHO. But available data on two indicators are telling. Loneliness among older adults (65+) in Taiwan stands at about 10% — similar to the least lonely countries (mostly in Northern Europe), better than North America (~20%), and far better than the PRC (30%+)[^Loneliness]. Self-reported cellphone addiction is about 28% in Taiwan, versus 58% in the US[^Addiction]. Drug use differences are even starker: roughly 10 times more Americans report using illegal drugs at least monthly than Taiwanese who have ever tried an illegal drug[^drugs].

Taiwan's religious landscape is also unusual. Most rich countries are dominated by one religious tradition and have seen sharp declines in religiosity. Taiwan has a roughly equal mix of folk religion, Taoism, Buddhism, Western and minority religions, and non-believers — and has seen little growth in non-belief or non-practice over recent decades[^religionTaiwan][^wikireligion][^GivingUp].

#### Political

Taiwan consistently ranks among the freest and most effective democracies in the world in indices published by Freedom House[^Freedom], the Economist Intelligence Unit[^EIU], the Bertelsmann Foundation, and V-Dem[^demrank]. It is almost always the strongest democracy in Asia and the strongest young democracy of reasonable size. And unlike most regions, where democracy has declined over the last decade, Taiwan's democratic scores have substantially increased.

Taiwan is also notable for its resistance to polarization. Multiple studies find Taiwan to be one of the least socially, ethnically, and religiously polarized developed countries — and consistently among the five least *affectively* polarized countries (meaning citizens hold the least hostile personal attitudes toward political opponents)[^polarization][^LeaderAffectivePolarization].

This is remarkable given that Taiwan is consistently identified as the world's most heavily targeted jurisdiction for disinformation[^disinfovolume]. Research by Bauer and Wilson offers one explanation: unlike in many other places, foreign manipulation in Taiwan tends to unite citizens against external interference rather than exacerbating partisan divisions[^Disinfo].

#### Legal

Taiwan is consistently ranked among the world's five safest countries and the safest democracy with more than 100,000 people by a large margin[^crime]. Crime continues to trend downward even as violent crime has surged in the US[^crimevus]. Taiwan achieves this with a strong police presence (somewhat higher than the US) and an incarceration rate that, while far below the US, is high by global standards.

Taiwan's legal-political system has also shown an ability to adapt and resolve long-standing social conflicts. In 2017, the Constitutional Court ruled that same-sex marriage must be legalized within two years. After a referendum on a straightforward same-sex marriage proposal failed in 2018, the government found a creative solution that addressed concerns from both sides. Many opponents worried that marriage traditions binding extended families would force reluctant kin to participate. Most young people who planned to marry under the new law had no desire to bind their families either. The government passed a legalization bill that exempted kin from the same-sex marriage process — satisfying both groups.

#### Existential

Crises are rare and hard to prepare for in advance. The closest proxy we have is COVID-19. Taiwan was widely seen as the world's best-performing country in the crisis's early stages — before vaccines were available. From the pandemic's start through April 2021, Taiwan suffered only 12 COVID deaths — by far the lowest rate among jurisdictions with internationally reliable data. It achieved this without lockdowns, while achieving the fastest economic growth of any rich country except Ireland in 2020. Taiwan's health system has been ranked the world's most efficient by Numbeo for most of the last decade, though its life expectancy is merely among the world's highest[^Numbeohealth].

[^Numbeohealth]: https://www.numbeo.com/health-care/rankings_by_country.jsp

It is important to note, however, that Taiwan performed less impressively in the post-crisis phase after mid-2021, when vaccine availability and uptake became critical. Challenges with domestic vaccine production and distribution led to significant additional deaths. Taiwan still had among the lowest death rates among large rich jurisdictions, but its early leadership did not fully persist. This may suggest that Taiwan's civic cohesion — triggered by crises like Sunflower and the pandemic — allows for exceptional initial responses, but that additional work is needed to institutionalize these capacities for the long term.

Climate change illustrates this challenge. Taiwan has enshrined 2050 net-zero targets in law and received praise for its plans, but progress has so far been modest[^climate][^epi].

[^climate]: "Net Zero Tracker," Energy & Climate Intelligence Unit, 2023. https://eciu.net/netzerotracker.
[^epi]: "2022 EPI Results," Environmental Performance Index, 2022, https://epi.yale.edu/epi-results/2022/component/epi.

Taiwan nonetheless shows unusually high institutional trust and democratic participation. Voter turnout is among the world's highest outside compulsory-voting countries[^turnout]. 91% of citizens consider democracy to be at least "fairly good" — a sharp contrast to the dramatic declines in democratic support seen in many long-established democracies[^demsupp].

[^turnout]: Drew DeSilver, "Turnout in U.S. Has Soared in Recent Elections but by Some Measures Still Trails that of Many Other Countries." Pew Research Center, November 1, 2022. https://www.pewresearch.org/short-reads/2022/11/01/turnout-in-u-s-has-soared-in-recent-elections-but-by-some-measures-still-trails-that-of-many-other-countries/.

[^demsupp]: "Taiwan Country Report Report," BTI Transformation Index, n.d., https://bti-project.org/en/reports/country-report/TWN.

Taiwan has important limitations like any country. But it deserves a leading place among global exemplars — a place it is too rarely afforded. The left admires Scandinavia; the right, Singapore. All have important lessons. But few places offer Taiwan's breadth of promise across today's leading challenges, or its appeal across typical political divides. As an economically free, vibrantly participatory liberal democracy, Taiwan has something to offer every point on the political spectrum. It also offers one of the most compelling examples of leapfrogging the practices of ailing Western democracies — especially given its starting point: no natural resources or strategic advantage, a fragile geopolitical setting, a deeply divided rather than homogeneous population, democratization only a few decades ago, and escape from poverty within a single century.

It will take decades of study to fully understand the causal connections between Taiwan's digital democratic practices and its successes. But in the interim, it seems critical to articulate the generalizable philosophy behind its strategies — as others have done for Scandinavia and Singapore. That is the task of the rest of this book.
