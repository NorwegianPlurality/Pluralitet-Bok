# A View From Yushan

> Swirling ocean, beautiful islands;  
> A transcultural republic of citizens.[^TROC]

---

[^TROC]: This is an alternate interpretation of 中華民國 (lit. "amidst" "cultures" "citizens" "nation"), usually translated as "Republic of China".

From the summit of East Asia's highest peak, [Yushan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yu_Shan) (Jade Mountain), you can look out across Taiwan and feel how this small mountain island sits at a global crossroads. Located where the Eurasian and Pacific tectonic plates meet, Taiwan is pushed upward each year by geological forces — while earthquakes test its resilient building codes. In the same way, Taiwan's clash of cultures, histories, and values has produced a prosperous, innovative society — and digital innovation has helped protect it from polarization.

Today, Taiwan has a voter turnout above 70%[^twelectionv], the second-highest religious diversity in the world[^ReligiousDiversityIndex], and produces 90% of the world's advanced chips. It has broken beyond its geographic limits and shown how a democratic society can collaborate with its region and the world.

[^twelectionv]: "Billing Profile Information," Central Election Commission, n.d, https://db.cec.gov.tw/ElecTable/Election?type=President.
[^ReligiousDiversityIndex]: Joseph Liu, "Global Religious Diversity," _Pew Research Center_, April 4, 2014. https://www.pewresearch.org/religion/2014/04/04/global-religious-diversity/.

Taiwan had one of the world's lowest COVID death rates with no lockdowns, while maintaining some of the fastest economic growth. Real-time mask maps and social distancing apps showed collaborative digital tools working in daily life.[^ExcessDeaths]

[^ExcessDeaths]: "Tracking Covid-19 Excess Deaths across Countries," The Economist, October 20, 2021. https://www.economist.com/graphic-detail/coronavirus-excess-deaths-tracker.

### Place of convergence

One etymology of Taiwan's name comes from the indigenous word "Taivoan," meaning "place of convergence." Taiwan may have been a launching point for long-distance cooperation longer than anywhere on earth — [believed](https://historylearning.com/history-of-the-philippines/pre-history/population-theories/out-of-taiwan-model/) to be the origin of Polynesian voyagers who traveled thousands of miles in the second millennium BCE[^Bellwood]. The island's story — shaped by indigenous cultures, colonial powers, and political ideologies — centers on the ongoing clash and co-creation between different ideas of what this place is and what it can be. This rich conflict has produced a unique form of democracy forged by constant upheaval.

[^Bellwood]: Peter Bellwood, _Man's Conquest of the Pacific: the Prehistory of Southeast Asia and Oceania_ (Oxford, UK: Oxford University Press, 1979).

Two experiences of this book's lead authors show this setting clearly. On March 18, 2014, students frustrated by a trade deal with Beijing climbed over the legislature fence. A similar US Capitol occupation almost seven years later lasted only hours and became one of America's most divisive events. Taiwan's "Sunflower" occupation lasted more than three weeks — and the protesters' demands were largely accepted. The movement changed the government and gave rise to new political parties.

Most importantly, the movement produced a lasting shift. The government gained respect for the movement; ministers invited younger "reverse mentors" to help them learn from youth and civil society. One proactive minister, Jaclyn Tsai — one of the world's first ministers for digital participation — recruited one of us into public service. This eventually led her to take that role in 2016 and become the first Minister of Digital Affairs in 2022.

Nearly a decade later, the other primary author visited for the general election of January 13, 2024. It opened a "year of elections" in which more people voted than ever before — following the "year of AI," when models like GPT entered public consciousness. Many feared AI would supercharge information manipulation by authoritarian actors. Taiwan seemed a test case: a better-funded adversary targeting a much smaller population[^VDemInfo]. Walking Taipei on the eve of the election, he found deep divisions. At the ruling DPP rally: no official flags, only island placards, green colors, and rainbow flags 🏳️‍🌈. At the opposition KMT rally: only the ROC flag 🇹🇼. He imagined how extreme America's divisions would seem if Democrats waved a British flag and Republicans the stars and stripes.

[^VDemInfo]: "Disinformation in Taiwan: International versus Domestic Perpetrators," V-Dem, 2020. https://v-dem.net/weekly_graph/disinformation-in-taiwan-international-versus

Yet despite these divides, the January 13 election became a positive model for the world. The candidate opposed by the authoritarian adversary outperformed polls. Calm prevailed. A broadly consensual outcome emerged. This capacity to channel divergent views toward shared progress through technology and social organization was most clearly shown in the decade after the Sunflower movement — but its roots go much deeper.

### Taiwan's historical lineage

The identities emphasized by the DPP and KMT reflect different visions of "this place." They resonate with another etymology: "tayw-an," meaning "people-place" in a related indigenous language (Siraya). For the KMT (associated with blue), Taiwan is defined by its Chinese-speaking majority — Mandarin, Taigi, and Hakka. Some argue Taiwan is more ethno-historically "Chinese" than the PRC: over 80% speak Mandarin as a primary language (versus 70% in PRC), over 40% follow Daoism (versus under 20% in PRC), and the official ideology is Tridemism, not Marxism. For those influenced by the DPP (associated with green), Taiwan is an island with a diverse, transcultural history — only two centuries as a Qing Chinese periphery — that should determine its own future. To understand these divides, we need to trace both the island's history and the ROC's.

The island's history is full of war, rebellion, colonizers, and independence movements. Like many South China Sea islands, indigenous peoples encountered imperial powers — Spanish, Japanese, Dutch — through colonial expansion. By the seventeenth century, the Dutch held the south and Spanish the north, both using the ports for trade while much of the interior remained inaccessible[^JJ1].

[^JJ1]: Emma Teng, _Taiwan's Imagined Geography: Chinese Colonial Travel Writing and Pictures, 1683-1895_, (Cambridge, Mass.: Harvard University Asia Center, 2004), 33.

South China Sea merchants and pirates from Japan, China, and Southeast Asia also settled on the island. In 1662, Zheng Chenggong (Koxinga), in rebellion against the new Qing dynasty (1644–1911), expelled the Dutch and continued fighting the Qing from Taiwan[^JJ2]. By 1683, the Qing had defeated the Zheng rebellion and Taiwan came nominally under Qing control.

[^JJ2]: Emma Teng, _Taiwan's Imagined Geography: Chinese Colonial Travel Writing and Pictures, 1683-1895_, (Cambridge, Mass.: Harvard University Asia Center, 2004), 33, 1-2.

Just over two hundred years later, in 1895, Qing's defeat in the Sino-Japanese War set two sequences of events in motion. First, Qing ceded Taiwan to Japan, beginning a half-century of Japanese colonial rule. Second, this defeat fueled a nationalist movement that created the ROC[^NationalismRoots].

[^NationalismRoots]: Suisheng Zhao, _The Dragon Roars Back: Transformational Leaders and Dynamics of Chinese Foreign Policy_, (Stanford, California: Stanford University Press, 2022), 132.

Under Japanese rule, a short-lived Formosa Republic was quickly suppressed at a cost of 12,000 lives. Japanese "assimilation" policy then tried to incorporate Taiwanese into the Japanese cultural and linguistic system, integrating language, government, urban planning, and elite education. Indigenous, Taigi, and Hakka peoples experienced this rule very differently depending on how "civilized" Japan considered them[^JJ10]. The global anti-colonial movement and Japan's Taishō democratic reforms gave Taiwanese intellectuals an ideological basis for self-determination. Local elections in 1935, open to a small fraction of property-owning men, gave elites a first taste of democratic participation[^JJ11].

[^JJ10]: Jeffrey Jacobs, _Democratizing Taiwan_, (Boston: Brill, 2012), 22.
[^JJ11]: Ashley Esarey, "Overview: Democratization and Nation Building in Taiwan" in _Taiwan in Dynamic Transition: Nation Building and Democratization_, edited by Thomas Gold, (Seattle: University of Washington Press, 2020), 24.

### Tridemism

Across the Taiwan Strait, Sun Yat-Sen — a young, American-educated, Christian doctor and activist — was also moved by Qing's defeat to Japan, but for different reasons. He concluded the dynasty was unreformable, led a series of failed uprisings, and fled to Japan, where he absorbed nascent democratic reform. Drawing on Japanese, Christian, American, and Confucian influences, Sun articulated his _[Three Principles of the People](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Three_Principles_of_the_People)_ in 1905, laying the foundation of "Tridemism" — the official philosophy and national anthem of the ROC.

The first principle is 民族/Mínzú (literally "civil clan"), usually translated as "nationalism." More notable was its emphasis on ethnic pluralism (五族共和), reflected in the original ROC flag, which included a color for each major ethnicity[^ROC1912Flag]. The second is 民權/Mínquán ("civil rights"), translated as "democracy" — a combination of election, recall, initiative, referendum, and a five-branch government system blending European and Confucian traditions. The third is 民生/Mínshēng ("civil livelihood"), translated as "socialism," drawing on multiple economic philosophies including the ideas of American economist Henry George, who advocated land rights equality, anti-monopoly stances, and cooperative enterprises. We return to these ideas extensively in the next part of the book.

[^ROC1912Flag]: "Flag of China (1912–1928)," n.d. Wikimedia Commons, https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Flag_of_China_(1912%E2%80%931928).svg.

Harnessing these ideas, Sun built international support and overthrew the Qing in 1911, founding the ROC in 1912. Internal conflict soon forced him back into exile and then into civil war. In 1919, he founded the modern KMT.

That year he also met another key influence: a disciple of Henry George visiting China to see how George's ideas might play out at scale. John Dewey was among the world's most respected philosophers of democracy. His "pragmatic" theory of democracy — translated by his Chinese student Hu Shih as "experimentalism" — resonated with the uncertain, exploratory atmosphere of the early ROC.

This fluid, experimental approach shared much with Daoist traditions popular among opponents of Qing rule[^TaoDewey]. Unlike many imperialistic foreign observers, Dewey encouraged the ROC to follow its own path of "collaborative problem solving." He gave over 200 lectures in China while writing monthly columns for outlets like _The New Republic_, helping forge a deep and lasting connection between the ROC and the US.

[^TaoDewey]: Richard Shusterman, "Pragmatism and East‐Asian Thought," _Metaphilosophy_ 35, no. 1-2 (2004): 13, https://www.academia.edu/3125320/_Pragmatism_and_East_Asian_Thought_.

The Russian Revolution brought financial support and military training to the Chinese Communist Party (CCP). Although inspired by a different — Marxist — vision of socialism, Sun allied with the communists to unify the country. This effort was nearly successful at his death in 1925, making Sun "Father of the Nation" for the Nationalists and "Forerunner of the Revolution" for the Communists.

That unity was short-lived. Communists (under Mao Zedong) and Nationalists (under Chiang Kai-shek) alternated between civil war and alliances against warlords and Japanese occupiers for the next twenty years, until Japan's defeat in 1945. Both sides focused so heavily on national liberation and fighting each other that Taiwan was largely an afterthought[^MaoTaiwan].

[^MaoTaiwan]: Yet, to the extent they did address it, Mao supported Taiwan as an independent communist state much as he hoped for Korea and Vietnam, while Chiang (almost as an afterthought) requested the return of Taiwan after the war along with other territories formerly occupied by Japan, including Manchuria.

### Postbellum Taiwan

[^JJ13]: Louzon Victor. "From Japanese Soldiers to Chinese Rebels: Colonial Hegemony, War Experience, and Spontaneous Remobilization during the 1947 Taiwanese Rebellion," _The Journal of Asian Studies_ 77, no. 1 (2018): 168.
[^JJ14]: Chien-Jung Hsu, _The Construction of National Identity in Taiwan's Media, 1896-2012_, (Leiden: Brill, 2014), 48.

In 1949, defeated by the Communists, Chiang Kai-shek and two million ROC soldiers and civilians fled to Taiwan. Declaring it the home of "free China," Chiang imposed martial law on the eight million native — mainly Taigi- and Hakka-speaking — population. This period became known as the "White Terror." As dictator, Chiang positioned the ROC as the true representative of China to the world. Inside Taiwan, people experienced a violent outsider government systematically suppressing Taiwanese identity[^JJ15].

[^JJ15]: Chien-Jung Hsu, _The Construction of National Identity in Taiwan's Media, 1896-2012_, (Leiden: Brill, 2014), 71.

At the same time, a government officially committed to Tridemism planted seeds of social reform that would later flower into democracy. Without ties to local elites, Chiang was able to impose rural land reform: a 37.5% rent cap in 1949, release of public land in 1951, and land redistribution in 1953. A Georgist land value tax followed in 1977. Scholars argue these reforms created an egalitarian economic foundation critical to Taiwan's later development[^AsiaWorks].

[^AsiaWorks]: Joe Studwell, "How Asia Works: Success and Failure in the World's Most Dynamic Region," (London: Profile, 2013).

Tridemism also drove support for cooperative enterprise, enshrined in the ROC Constitution. This drew on Georgist ideas and traditions of agricultural and industrial cooperation from Japanese colonial rule, further influenced by American thinker Edward Deming, who emphasized empowering workers to improve production[^EdwardDeming]. Together, these influences fostered a robust civil and cooperative sector in Taiwan — what we call the Third Sector — critical to its industrial and political future. Public investment in export infrastructure also propelled Taiwan's rise. By the 1970s, Taiwan was a major supplier of components for advanced Western technologies.

Taiwan's education system was similarly shaped by the early ROC's intellectual ferment. Dewey's student Hu Shih fled to Taiwan with the KMT. As president of Academia Sinica and a leading intellectual, Hu shaped Taiwan's educational system with a blend of Confucian tradition and Deweyian pragmatism and egalitarianism — helping Taiwan top world education league tables across multiple benchmarks[^TaiwanEd].

[^TaiwanEd]: "John Dewey and Free China," Taiwan Today, January 1, 2003, https://taiwantoday.tw/news.php?unit=12.

### Coming of democracy

The 1960s — parallel to the American Civil Rights movement — saw new demands for independence and genuine democracy in Taiwan. Professor Peng Ming-min and two students circulated the Taiwan Self-Salvation Manifesto, calling for a free and independent Taiwan and rejecting the ROC as illegitimate[^JJ18]. Peng was exiled, but the manifesto sparked a national conversation that pushed democratic advocates to demand national elections.

[^JJ18]: Ryan Dunch, and Ashley Esarey, _Taiwan in Dynamic Transition: Nation-Building and Democratization_, (Seattle: University Of Washington Press, 2020), 28.

The United Nations was central to the ROC's early post-war identity. The ROC was a founding UN member and the only Asian permanent Security Council member — a constant irritant to the PRC, which could not participate in international affairs as a result. This led the CCP to shift from supporting Taiwanese independence to focusing on conquering Taiwan. As the US sought to limit its failures in Vietnam, President Nixon secretly pursued accommodation with the PRC. He supported UN General Assembly Resolution 2758 in 1971, which transferred recognition of "China" from the ROC to the PRC, culminating in his 1972 visit. The ROC "withdrew" from the UN, fundamentally changing its identity.

This withdrawal greatly limited Taiwan's international activities and trade. It also led the US and most of the non-Communist world to shift from unconditional support for the ROC to careful balancing — seeking to prevent PRC violence against Taiwan while acknowledging China's "One China" position.

Internally, this identity shift undermined the rationale for the White Terror. The idea of "free China" retaking the mainland through US support faded. The contradictions between Taiwan's increasingly egalitarian, educated, Third Sector-driven society and its authoritarian government became overwhelming — especially as labor unions, political associations, and Chiang's death all converged before the end of the 1970s. The parents of one of this book's authors illustrate these trends: as pioneers of community college and cooperative movements, they benefited from the cooperative support in the ROC constitution. As journalists, they covered the repression, including the 1979 Kaohsiung Incident where opposition leaders were imprisoned, building the foundation for democratization.

Taiwan's weakened international position allowed White Terror exiles to pressure Chiang's son and successor, Chiang Ching-Kuo. His liberalization of Taiwan in the 1980s created space for democratic action — protests, essays, songs, art — reflecting a growing demand for general elections. Democratic advocates were still jailed or in exile, but their relatives and friends began running for office[^JJ22].

[^JJ22]: Ryan Dunch, and Ashley Esarey, _Taiwan in Dynamic Transition: Nation-Building and Democratization_, (Seattle: University Of Washington Press, 2020), 31.

### Vibrant democratic generation

In 1984, Chiang Ching-Kuo selected Lee Teng-hui as Taiwan's first Taiwan-born vice president, signaling a political shift[^JJ23]. When Lee became President in 1988, he quickly pushed democratic reforms — including direct presidential elections and placing sovereignty in the "citizens of the Free Area" of the ROC. He became the first directly elected President in 1996, just months after Bill Gates's "Internet Tidal Wave" memo heralded the mainstream internet age.

[^JJ23]: Jeffrey Jacobs, _Democratizing Taiwan_, (Boston: Brill, 2012), 62.

Already one of the world's most technology-intensive export economies, Taiwan was swept by this digital wave with the same force as democratization. The internet and democracy were almost twins in Taiwan. Four years later, the first DPP President, Chen Shui-bian, narrowly won election as the Blue camp split. When the KMT returned to the presidency in 2008, a pattern of alternation between the Blue vision of "free China" and the Green vision of "island nation" was established.

Yet despite the deep division that fueled the Sunflower movement, the overlap between these two visions is striking:

1. **Pluralism:** Both the Blue and Green stories emphasize pluralism. Blue celebrates fusing contemporary and traditional culture (symbolized by the National Palace Museum) and Tridemist pluralism. Green focuses on the diversity of Taiwan's settlers — indigenous peoples, Japanese, Hokkien, Hakka, Westerners, and new immigrants.
2. **Diplomatic nuance:** Navigating the relationship with the PRC has required both sides to hold complex positions on US security commitments, the meaning of "ROC" and "Taiwan," and the concept of "independence."
3. **Democratic freedom:** Both ideologies center on democracy and freedom. For Greens, these are the rallying cries against both the White Terror and PRC authoritarianism. For Blues, they are the core of Tridemism.
4. **Anti-authoritarianism:** Both are deeply concerned about growing PRC authoritarianism, especially after the failure of "One Country, Two Systems" in Hong Kong.
5. **Export-orientation:** Both celebrate Taiwan as a commercial exporter and see the ability to export ideas and culture as central to its future. Blues focus more on influencing the PRC; Greens on gaining respect in the "free world."

Beyond this ideological overlap, both sides have been shaped by Taiwan's central role in global electronics. As the hub of the semiconductor and smartphone supply chain, with the world's fastest internet[^Statista], no country is more thoroughly immersed in the digital world than Taiwan.

[^Statista]: Taiwan News, "Taiwan Has No. 1 Fastest Internet in World," October 23, 2023. https://www.taiwannews.com.tw/en/news/5025449.

This combination — a shared commitment to plural, complex, free, world-facing democracy, with easy access to digital tools to navigate its ambiguities — has made Taiwan, over the last decade, the world's leading example of digital democracy.
