# What is ⿻?

> "Action, the only activity that goes on directly between men without the intermediary of things or matter, corresponds to the human condition of plurality, to the fact that men, not Man, live on the earth and inhabit the world." - Hannah Arendt, *The Human Condition*, 1958[^Arendt]

> "(A)n ideal of 'social connectedness'...denotes a society where bridging ties, across lines of difference are formed at a high rate." - Danielle Allen, "Towards a Connected Society", 2017[^Allen]

> "Democracy is a technology. Like any technology, it gets better when more people strive to improve it." - Audrey Tang, Interview with Azeem Azhar, 2020[^Audrey]

---

The growing tension between democracy and technology — and Taiwan's apparent success in overcoming it — raises a natural question: is there a broader lesson here about how technology and democracy can work together?

We tend to think of technology as something that relentlessly marches forward, while democracy is a static choice among competing forms of social organization. Taiwan's experience suggests there are more options. Technology can be more like politics: open to different directions. One of those directions could radically improve how we live and collaborate — advancing democracy the way we advance technology. It also shows that social differences need not only cause conflict; with the right technology, they can drive progress.

This kind of thinking is not entirely new. The most beloved science fiction franchise, *Star Trek*, gives its heroic Vulcans a philosophy of "Infinite Diversity in Infinite Combinations...a belief that beauty, growth, progress — all result from the union of the unlike." In that spirit, we define "⿻ 數位 Plurality" — the subject of the rest of this book — as **technology for collaboration across social difference**.

This contrasts with what both Libertarianism and Technocracy share: a view of the world as made up of individuals (atoms) and a social whole. We call this "monist atomism." Both disagree about how much authority should go to each level, but both miss the core idea of ⿻: that intersecting, diverse social groups — and the people whose identities are shaped by these intersections — are the basic fabric of the social world.

⿻ has three components — descriptive, normative, and prescriptive — each associated with one of three thinkers (Hannah Arendt, Danielle Allen, and Audrey Tang), as shown in Figure A:

1. **Descriptive: The social world is neither a collection of isolated individuals nor a single unified whole. It is a fabric of diverse, overlapping affiliations that define both personal identities and collective organization.** We identify this with Hannah Arendt and especially her book *The Human Condition*, where she calls Plurality the most fundamental element of the human condition. We represent it with the Unicode character ⿻, which captures the overlapping, intersectional nature of both individual and group identity. In the next chapter, [Living in a ⿻ World](https://www.plurality.net/v/chapters/3-1/eng/?mode=dark), we show this applies not just to human society but — according to modern complexity science — to essentially all complex phenomena in nature.

2. **Normative: Diversity is the fuel of social progress. Like any fuel, it can explode into conflict — but societies succeed when they harness its potential energy for growth.** We identify this with philosopher Danielle Allen's ideal of "A Connected Society," represented by the rainbow elements at the intersections of the ⿻ image. While Allen has given perhaps the clearest expression of these ideas, they are rooted in a deeper philosophical tradition including Henry George and John Dewey — thinkers who deeply influenced Taiwan.

3. **Prescriptive: Digital technology should build the engines that harness diversity's energy and prevent its explosion — just as industrial technology built engines that harnessed physical fuel and contained its explosions.** We identify this with one of us using the term "Plurality" from 2016 to name a technological agenda. Even more closely, we associate it with the traditional Mandarin characters 數位 (pronounced "shuwei"), which in Taiwan mean both "plural" (applied to people) and "digital." This captures the fusion of Arendt's and Allen's philosophy with the transformative potential of digital technology. In the final chapter of this section, we argue that this philosophy — though never fully articulated — drove much of what became the internet, and that its loss since then explains much of what has gone wrong. A primary goal of the rest of the book is to state this vision clearly, so it can become a real alternative to the Libertarian, Technocratic, and stagnant democratic stories that dominate today's debate.

Throughout the rest of the book, we use the Unicode ⿻ to represent this idea set, both as a noun (standing in for "Plurality") and as an adjective (standing in for 數位).

In English it can be read in different ways depending on context:

- As "Plurality" when used as a concept[^Taiwan]
- As "digital," "plural," "shuwei," "digital/plural," "intersectional," "collaborative," or "networked" when used as an adjective

No existing English word perfectly captures this idea. In some contexts, "overlap" or "overlapping" works as a literal description. The rest of the book explores the full content, vision, and ambition of ⿻.

[^Taiwan]: Note that ⿻ could also represent the overlapping meanings of the two standard variations on the name of Audrey's jurisdiction — ROC and Taiwan — avoiding the need for conflict between them. We leave this for someone else to develop, to avoid creating too much ambiguity in ⿻'s meaning.
