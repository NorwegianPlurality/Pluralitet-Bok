# Information Technology and Democracy: a Widening Gulf

> "Surveillance capitalism is...a coup from above...an overthrow of the people's sovereignty and a prominent force in the perilous drift towards democratic deconsolidation..." — Shoshana Zuboff, [The Age of Surveillance Capitalism](https://www.hachettebookgroup.com/titles/shoshana-zuboff/the-age-of-surveillance-capitalism/9781610395694/?lens=publicaffairs)[^surveillancecapitalism], 2019

Anxiety about technology and geopolitics is everywhere today. But there is a deeper conflict than the rivalry between great powers. Technology and democracy have been moving in opposite directions — and the fight is hurting both sides.

The biggest trends in technology over the past few decades have been artificial intelligence and blockchains. AI has strengthened centralized, top-down control. Blockchains have fueled atomized, polarizing financial capitalism. Both undermine democratic values. It is no surprise that technology is widely seen as one of the biggest threats to democracy — a tool for authoritarians and for those trying to undermine democracies from within.

At the same time, democracy was once a bold experiment: scaling city-state governance to millions of citizens across continents. A [quote](https://www.monticello.org/research-education/thomas-jefferson-encyclopedia/quotations-jefferson-memorial/) on the Thomas Jefferson Memorial in Washington, D.C. reads: "(L)aws and institutions must go hand in hand with the progress of the human mind... We might as well require a man to wear still the coat which fitted him when a boy as civilized society to remain ever under the regimen of their barbarous ancestors." Yet today, democracy often means desperate efforts to preserve rigid, outdated, paralyzed governments. It is no wonder many technologists see democratic participation as an obstacle to progress, and many democracy advocates fear that technology will hand victory to authoritarians.

In this book, we want to show that this conflict is avoidable. Technology and democracy can be powerful allies. But bridging the gap requires honestly acknowledging the concerns on both sides. We start by laying out those grievances clearly.

### Technology's attack on democracy

In the last decade, information technology has threatened democracy in two opposite ways. As Daron Acemoglu and James A. Robinson argued, free democratic societies exist in a "[narrow corridor](https://www.penguinrandomhouse.com/books/555400/the-narrow-corridor-by-daron-acemoglu-and-james-a-robinson/)" between social collapse and authoritarianism[^NarrowCorridor]. Technology is narrowing that corridor from both sides.

On one side, technologies like social media, cryptography, and financial tech are breaking down social cohesion. They increase polarization, erode norms, undermine law enforcement, and accelerate financial markets beyond democratic accountability. We call these "anti-social" threats. On the other side, technologies like machine learning, foundation models, and the internet of things are increasing the power of centralized surveillance and letting small groups of engineers shape the rules of life for billions of people. We call these "centralizing" threats. Both strike at the heart of democracy, which — as Alexis de Tocqueville argued in [Democracy in America](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/815/815-h/815-h.htm) — depends on diverse, non-market, decentralized social connections[^Tocqueville].

The anti-social threat shows up in several areas:

- **Socially**, social media has helped isolated minorities find community. But [on average](https://doi.org/10.1257/aer.20190658) it has increased [social isolation](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.chbr.2021.100070) and feelings of exclusion[^OutInTheCountry].

- **Economically**, the internet has created new flexibility for workers in developing countries and non-traditional labor markets. But [labor institutions have not kept up](https://ghostwork.info/) — no new unions, no new protections. The result has been more job insecurity and the "[hollowing out](https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257%2Fjep.29.3.3&source=post_page)" of the middle class in many developed countries[^GhostWork].

- **Politically**, polarization and extremism have been rising in many democracies. The internet's role is [debated by academics](https://www.pnas.org/doi/abs/10.1073/pnas.1706588114), but surveys suggest social media has done little to strengthen ties across differences — and may well have driven rising polarization since 2000, especially in the US[^PolarizationResearch].

- **Legally**, financial innovation has produced few measurable benefits for consumers while increasing systemic risk and enabling fraud, tax evasion, and regulatory evasion. Crypto assets are the clearest example[^FinancialInnovation][^CryptoChallenges].

- **Existentially**, the fragmentation of shared sense-making is dangerous given the growing power of technologies of mass destruction — from climate change to bioweapons to misaligned AI[^TechnologySocietyImpact].

But even as technology erodes democratic cohesion, it also concentrates power in fewer hands:

- **Socially**, technology has made vast amounts of personal information public. The ability to process that information has concentrated in corporations with privileged data access and the capital to build large AI systems. Societies where central actors control large data pools and capital have pulled ahead in the "AI race," normalizing surveillance and centralized information control[^SurveillanceCapitalism].

- **Legally**, AI has outpaced core democratic rights. Intellectual property law can't handle AI remixing content. Privacy law can't keep up with data spread. Anti-discrimination law is ill-suited to black-box AI bias. The engineers making these systems come overwhelmingly from a narrow demographic and social background, working for profit-seeking companies or defense[^AIChallenges].

- **Economically**, AI and information technology broadly have replaced rather than complemented human labor — especially for lower-skilled workers. This has driven a rising share of income going to capital rather than labor, increasing inequality and market power in technology-heavy sectors[^AIandInequality][^MarketPower].

- **Geo-politically**, these forces have strengthened authoritarian regimes. Mass surveillance tools, AI, and large-scale data processing have made censorship and social control easier. Concentrating economic power and social levers in a few corporate chokepoints makes it easier for authoritarian governments to seize or manipulate those centers of power[^AuthoritarianTech].

These two threats feed each other. Authoritarian regimes harness social media chaos to deepen divisions in democracies. Centralized platforms use AI to maximize engagement, fueling polarization. Together or separately, both forces have pressured democracies and undermined confidence in them — confidence now at its [lowest point](https://www.pewtrusts.org/en/trust/archive/spring-2022/global-public-opinion-in-an-era-of-democratic-anxiety) since measurement began.[^Demdecline]

[^Demdecline]: Richard Wike and Jannell Fetterolf, "Global Public Opinion in an Era of Democratic Anxiety" *Pew Trust Magazine* May 27, 2022. Ironically, in fragile democracies where the state has limited capacity for technology governance, chaos (the collapse of the prevailing order through disruptive technologies) could be an ally of democracy. From the Arab Spring that swept across North Africa in the early 2010s to Nigeria's #EndSARS movement in 2020, autocracies and fragile democracies have recorded the rise of an emerging class of social media-savvy, financial technology (fintech)-enabled and cryptocurrency-empowered class of young citizens deploying these technologies to challenge authoritarian state institutions. These disruptors have been aided by the algorithms of the technology companies, albeit to the extent that the objectives of such social movements align with the commercial interests of the corporations. Michael Etter and Oana Albu, "Activists in the Dark: Social Media Algorithms and Collective Action in Two Social Movement Organizations." _Organization_ 28, no. 1 (September 29, 2020): 135050842096153. https://doi.org/10.1177/1350508420961532. In some cases, such movements have been boosted by the explicit endorsement and backing of the influential founder. Jack Dorsey (@Jack) "Donate via #Bitcoin to help #EndSARS 🇳🇬…," X, October 14, 2020, 10.05pm, https://twitter.com/jack/status/1316485283777519620?  Without a doubt, such interventions foster democratic movements and amplify the otherwise repressed voices of citizens. However, aside from the risk of poor understanding of context and potential divisiveness that such foreign interventions could be prone to, they underscore the debate around the impact of non-state actors such as the global corporation on state sovereignty in Africa and the global South by extension. Ohimai Amaize, _How Twitter Amplified the Divisions That Derailed Nigeria's #EndSARS Movement_, Slate Magazine, April 20, 2021, https://slate.com/technology/2021/04/endsars-nigeria-twitter-jack-dorsey-feminist-coalition.html.

### Democracies' hostility to technology

The hostility has not been one-sided. Democracies have grown increasingly hostile to technology. Where democratic governments once led the world in building information technology — the early computers, the internet, GPS satellites — they are now mostly focused on restricting it. They are failing to seize the opportunities or meet the challenges it creates.

This failure shows up in four ways. First, public opinion and policymakers have turned against large tech companies — the "techlash." Second, democratic countries have sharply reduced direct investment in information technology. Third, they have been slow to adopt technology in public services. Fourth, democratic governments have largely failed to address the areas where technologists agree public support and regulation are essential[^DemocracyTechHostility].

Public attitudes turned sharply negative in the 2010s. Social media and the internet were once celebrated for openness. By the late 2010s, they were blamed for many social ills[^PublicOpinionTech]. This shift was reflected in bestselling books like *[Weapons of Math Destruction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Weapons_of_Math_Destruction)* and *[The Age of Surveillance Capitalism](https://www.hachettebookgroup.com/titles/shoshana-zuboff/the-age-of-surveillance-capitalism/9781610395694/?lens=publicaffairs)*, and in films like [The Social Dilemma](https://www.thesocialdilemma.com/). The techlash — shown in Figure A — became a defining political trend.

Regulators in the EU and US responded with antitrust actions, the GDPR, the Digital Markets Act, and other interventions. These have clear rationales. But the overall tone of hostility, disconnected from any positive technology vision, has created the impression of an industry under siege.

The clearest quantitative sign of declining public interest in technology is falling government R&D spending as a share of GDP. In most developed democracies, public R&D spending has declined even as business spending has grown and China's government spending has risen sharply, focused on information technology[^TechInvestmentDecline]. Figure B shows the US example.

The public sector once built the internet and the personal computer. Today, almost all major technology breakthroughs are private sector-driven[^PublicInterestTech].

The original internet was built by the public and academic sectors on open standards. "Web 2.0," "web3," and decentralized social technologies have received almost no public funding, even as democratic governments struggle to develop digital currencies, payments, and identity systems. While wartime democratic governments drove the computing breakthroughs of the 20th century, governments played almost no role in the "foundation model" revolution that is now reshaping computer science. OpenAI founders Sam Altman and Elon Musk [report](https://www.nytimes.com/2021/06/11/podcasts/transcript-ezra-klein-interviews-sam-altman.html) initially seeking government funding, only turning to private capital after repeated rejections[^AltmanInterview]. This contrasts sharply with authoritarian regimes like China and the UAE, which have built ambitious public AI strategies and [cutting-edge competitors to GPT models](https://falconllm.tii.ae/)[^TechStrategyPRC].

This disengagement extends to digital services and connectivity. Many wealthy democracies invest less in e-government than authoritarian counterparts. The UN's e-government index for 2022 ranked authoritarian UAE 13th and Kazakhstan 28th — ahead of Canada (32nd), Italy (37th), Brazil (49th), and Mexico (62nd)[^EGDI].

[^EGDI]:_United Nations Department of Economic and Social Affairs_. E-Government Knowledge Database, 2022 available at https://publicadministration.un.org/egovkb/Data-Center

Connectivity shows the same pattern, though the data are mixed. Many high-functioning democracies, such as the Scandinavian countries, have high quality and widely available internet. But leading authoritarian regimes dramatically outperform democracies at similar development levels. According to Speedtest.net, the PRC ranks 16th in the world in internet speeds while ranking only 72nd in income per head; Saudi Arabia and other Gulf monarchies similarly punch above their weight[^DigitalDisconnect]. The gap is more dramatic on 5G, the latest generation of mobile connectivity: a range of surveys find Saudi Arabia and the PRC consistently among the 10 best-covered jurisdictions, far above their income levels.

The most striking gap is digital public goods. Communication and knowledge now flow mainly through social media and search engines. The public square has moved online. But democratic countries have almost entirely failed to support digital public infrastructure. The non-profit [Mastodon](https://joinmastodon.org/) runs on Patreon donations. Open source software and Wikipedia are critical public goods but receive almost no government support[^Mastodonsupport]. Meanwhile authoritarian regimes [forge ahead](https://www.atlanticcouncil.org/cbdctracker/) with central bank digital currencies while most democracies are still exploring the idea.

[^Mastodonsupport]: Sara Perez, "Amid Twitter chaos, Mastodon grew donations 488% in 2022, reached 1.8M monthly active users", *Tech Crunch*, October 2, 2023 at https://techcrunch.com/2023/10/02/amid-twitter-chaos-mastodon-grew-donations-488-in-2022-reached-1-8m-monthly-active-users/)

Authoritarian regimes have also built cities and redesigned regulations to enable experiments with new technologies — driverless cars in Shenzhen, smart cities in Saudi Arabia. The PRC has gone further, building a detailed national technology strategy covering nearly every aspect of policy, regulation and investment[^TechInvestmentPRC]. In democratic countries, even modest projects like Google's Sidewalk Labs have been blocked by local opposition[^Sideways].

[^Sideways]: Josh O'Kane, *Sideways: The City Google Couldn't Buy* (Toronto: Random House Canada, 2022).

Even where technologists agree that regulation is essential — for catastrophic AI risk, systemic crypto risk, bioweapons — democratic governments have barely begun to engage. Labor law is ill-suited to flexible digital work. Copyright cannot handle AI attribution. Blockchain governance challenges existing securities law.

And the most fundamental democratic mechanism — voting — has barely changed in a century. Citizens communicate instantly across the planet but are represented in fixed geographic configurations at great expense and low quality. Modern communication and data tools play almost no role in democratic life.

Meanwhile, authoritarian regimes are using facial recognition to track protesters, digital payments to monitor financial activity, and social credit systems to rate citizens. Democracy is falling behind technology as much because it has ignored technology as because technology itself is anti-democratic[^SingleRating][^RussianDigitalControl][^RussianDraftEvaders].

### You get what you pay for

How did we get here? Is this the natural path of technology and democracy? Can it be different?

Evidence suggests that technology and democracy can co-evolve in many different ways. The path most democracies are on reflects specific choices: policies, attitudes, expectations, and culture.

Science fiction reveals the range of futures the human mind can imagine — and often shapes what researchers actually build. Neal Stephenson's 1992 novel [*Snow Crash*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Snow_Crash) imagines most people retreating into an immersive "metaverse" while real-world communities collapse and mafias take over[^Crash]. That future closely resembles the "anti-social" threats described above. Meta Platforms takes its name from Stephenson's metaverse. Other fictions explore AI concentrating power, as in the work of [Asimov](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Three_Laws_of_Robotics), [Iain M. Banks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Culture_series), and films like [Terminator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Terminator)[^AISciFi].

[^Crash]: Neal Stephenson, *Snow Crash* (New York: Bantam, 1992).

[^AISciFi]: Isaac Asimov, *I, Robot* (New York: Gnome Press: 1950). Iain M. Banks, *Consider Phlebas* (London: Macmillan, 1987). Ray Kurzweil, *The Age of Spiritual Machines* (New York: Viking, 1999). Nicholas Bostrom, *Superintelligence* (Oxford, UK: Oxford University Press, 2014).

But these are only some of the possibilities. *The Jetsons* imagines technology reinforcing 1950s America. *Star Trek* imagines a post-capitalist world of diverse alien intelligences. Ursula Le Guin imagined post-gender, post-state societies. Octavia Butler explored post-colonial futures. The range is vast[^ScienceFiction].

This diversity of possible futures is also the central conclusion of the field of Science and Technology Studies: the direction of technology is not fixed. It is shaped by collective choices[^STS]. Two leading economists, Daron Acemoglu and Simon Johnson, have recently argued that the direction of technological progress is a key policy target, not a given[^PowerProgress].

Comparing countries today makes the point most clearly. China's AI surveillance, Russia's hacking networks, web3 communities, Western capitalist technology, and the digital democracies of India, Estonia, and Taiwan all look radically different. Technology is not converging — it is proliferating possible futures.

So if our current path is not inevitable, how are we choosing it? The most concrete answer lies in investment choices. Western liberal democracies have directed their technological investments for the past half-century mainly toward AI and crypto-adjacent "web3." Yet it is striking that the most important technologies of recent decades — personal computers, the internet, mobile phones, social networks, remote work — fit neither the AI nor the crypto story. They were tools that augmented human creativity and connection. As we will show, they came from a very different tradition. Even the few genuine leaps of the last half-century often ran counter to the dominant ideologies.

### Ideologies of the twenty-first century

If technology's direction can be shaped by collective choices, how should we think about those choices? One useful analogy is ideologies. Societies choose to organize themselves through different combinations of communism, capitalism, democracy, fascism, theocracy, and so on. Each has strengths, weaknesses, and prerequisites.

We can look at technology trajectories the same way. The range is not unlimited — some things are easier, harder, or impossible. But neither is it fixed. There are clusters of plausible visions and technologies that support them. Through investment choices, societies help determine which futures are built.

The game series [Civilization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Civilization_(series)) captures this well. In [Civilization VI](https://civilization.com/)'s "Information Era," players choose between three ideologies: "[Synthetic Technocracy](https://civilization.fandom.com/wiki/Synthetic_Technocracy_(Civ6))," "[Corporate Libertarianism](https://civilization.fandom.com/wiki/Corporate_Libertarianism_(Civ6))," and "[Digital Democracy](https://civilization.fandom.com/wiki/Digital_Democracy_(Civ6))." We use these three — shortened below — to describe the main ideological divide of our time.

#### Artificial Intelligence and technocracy

The most widely held vision today centers on AI. Technocracy imagines AI creating what OpenAI founder Sam Altman calls "[Moore's Law for Everything](https://moores.samaltman.com/)": AI makes all material goods cheap and abundant, potentially abolishing material scarcity[^Moore]. But this abundance may concentrate in the hands of those who control AI. So technocracy typically calls for material redistribution — usually a universal basic income (UBI) — and strong centralized control over who can develop powerful AI. The concept of "Artificial General Intelligence" (AGI) — machines that exceed human capability broadly — is central.

[^Moore]: Sam Altman, "Moore's Law for Everything", March 16, 2021 https://moores.samaltman.com/.

Leading proponents in Silicon Valley include Sam Altman, Reid Hoffman, and (until recently) Elon Musk. The view is also prominent in China, reflected in its official AI Development Plan. In science fiction, it appears in the work of Asimov, Banks, Kurzweil, and Bostrom — with Bostrom's *[Deep Utopia](https://nickbostrom.com/deep-utopia/)* as perhaps the purest expression[^Deep]. Organizations aligned with this view include OpenAI and DeepMind. In US politics, Andrew Yang brought these ideas into the mainstream.

[^Deep]: Nicholas Bostrom, *Deep Utopia: Life and Meaning in a Solved World* (Washington, DC: Ideapress, 2024).

#### Crypto and hyper-capitalism

A second vision — less mainstream but influential in crypto and online communities — is captured by "Corporate Libertarianism," which we shorten to "Libertarianism."

Libertarianism imagines cryptography and networking protocols replacing human collective politics. Individuals would participate in free markets without government regulation or "coercion." The fictional inspirations are [Ayn Rand](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atlas_Shrugged) and Stephenson. Stephenson's *Snow Crash* and *Cryptonomicon* (1999), though intended as warnings, have been adopted as blueprints[^Rand][^CryptoStephenson]. Key technologies include immersive virtual worlds, private digital currencies, and strong cryptography. Bitcoin, Web3, and 4Chan communities form its social base.

[^Rand]: Ayn Rand, *Atlas Shrugged* (New York: Random House, 1957).
[^CryptoStephenson]: Neal Stephenson, *Cryptonomicon* (New York: Avon, 1999).

The Libertarian canon includes *[The Sovereign Individual](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Sovereign_Individual)*, the [writings](https://www.unqualified-reservations.org/) of Curtis Yarvin, [*The Network State*](https://thenetworkstate.com/), and *[Bronze Age Mindset](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bronze_Age_Pervert)*[^NRX]. Venture capitalist Peter Thiel is widely seen as its central intellectual leader.

[^NRX]: James Dale Davidson and Lord William Rees-Mogg, *The Sovereign Individual: Mastering the Transition to the Information Age* (New York: Touchstone, 1999).  Mencius Moldbug, *Unqualified Reservations* https://www.unqualified-reservations.org/. Balaji Srinavasan, *The Network State* (Self-published, 2022) available at https://thenetworkstate.com/.  Bronze Age Pervert, *Bronze Age Mindset* (Self-published, 2018).

Libertarianism has a complex relationship with the nationalist right. Many followers support it politically, and Thiel became a primary financial backer of Donald Trump. But Libertarianism is consistently hostile to nationalism itself, and followers often mock traditional right-wing values. This contradiction may reflect shared hostility to perceived left-wing cultural dominance, or an "accelerationist" view — shared by Yarvin and Davidson — that treats nationalist backlash as a useful accelerant for dissolving the nation-state.

### Stagnation and inequality

These two ideologies have dominated public thinking about technology in most liberal democracies for the last half-century. AI hype was nearly as intense in the 1980s as it is today (see Figure E). Libertarianism peaked in the 1990s, with John Perry Barlow's "[Declaration of the Independence of Cyberspace](https://www.eff.org/cyberspace-independence)", Stephenson's novels, and *The Sovereign Individual*.

Their radical promises led many to expect dramatic economic and productivity growth. Those promises have not been kept.

Instead of an explosion of economic growth, the last half-century has seen a dramatic slowdown in productivity. Figure F shows growth in US "Total Factor Productivity" (TFP) — economists' broadest measure of technological improvement — by decade. Rates during the mid-century "Golden Age" roughly doubled those before and after, during what we call the "Digital Stagnation." The pattern is even more stark in Europe and democratic Asia — South Korea and Taiwan are notable exceptions.

Worse, stagnation has come with rising inequality. Figure G shows average income growth by income percentile in the US, comparing the Golden Age and the Digital Stagnation. During the Golden Age, growth was roughly equal across the distribution, with top earners trailing slightly. During the Digital Stagnation, higher earners benefited far more — with only the top 1% exceeding Golden Age average growth rates.

Why has the last half-century performed so poorly compared to the one before? Economists point to many factors: rising market power, declining unions, the diminishing returns of innovation. But growing evidence focuses on two: the shift in technology toward automating rather than augmenting human labor, and the shift in policy toward "free markets know best" rather than active industrial shaping.

On the first point, Acemoglu, Restrepo, and collaborators show that during the Golden Age, labor augmentation roughly balanced automation. During the Digital Stagnation, automation slightly accelerated while augmentation collapsed. The result: slower productivity growth and a much smaller share of income going to workers, concentrated among lower-skilled workers[^AcemogluRestrepoStudy]. Figure H summarizes these results.

The role of "neoliberal" policy in this story is widely debated. We refer readers to related writing for a review of the evidence[^PosnerWeylBook][^PhilipponBook]. But clearly, the defining direction of this period was an embrace of market capitalism — closely tied to the Libertarian idea that globalization makes collective governance impossible. The last half-century has been defined by Technocracy in technology and Libertarianism in policy. Both have largely failed.

The biggest real breakthroughs of this period — personal computers, the internet, smartphones — fit neither vision. They augmented human creativity rather than automating it — what is often called "intelligence augmentation," or IA, rather than AI[^IA]. They enabled communication and connection rather than private markets. They came from a different tradition. Even the genuine technological leaps of the Digital Stagnation largely ran counter to its dominant ideologies.

### A fraying social contract

The economic damage is the easiest part to measure. Deeper, more insidious, and ultimately more damaging has been the erosion of trust — in democracy and in technology.

Faith in democratic institutions has fallen across most democracies, especially in the US, where dissatisfaction has gone from a fringe view (under 25%) to the majority opinion over the past 30 years[^CambridgeDemocracySurvey]. Faith in technology companies has similarly declined. The US tech sector has fallen from the most trusted sector in the economy in the early 2010s to among the least trusted[^EdelmanTrustBarometer].

This extends to a general collapse of confidence in social institutions. The fraction of Americans expressing high confidence in major institutions — religion, government, schools, media, law enforcement — has roughly halved since such surveys began[^GallupInstitutionConfidence]. Europe's decline has been more gradual, but the trend is broadly accepted[^TrustInPublicInstitutions].

### Reclaiming our future

Technology and democracy are trapped on opposite sides of a widening gulf. The conflict is hurting both — slowing growth, deepening inequality, and eroding trust in institutions. But this conflict is not inevitable. It is the result of the technological directions liberal democracies have chosen to invest in, driven by ideologies hostile to democratic values.

Another path is possible. Technology and democracy can be each other's greatest allies. In fact, a genuine "Digital Democracy" — one that has barely been imagined, let alone built — requires unprecedented technology to work. By shifting public investments, research agendas, and private development, we can build that future. In the rest of this book, we show you how. We begin by telling the story of a place that has gone further than anywhere else in realizing it.
